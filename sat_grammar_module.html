Research Background

Purpose of This Module

Understanding why this module exists helps you connect the learning to what really matters.

The Instructional Problem

Many 11th grade students can recognize parts of speech in a simple sentence — but struggle when grammar questions appear inside a longer reading passage, which is exactly how the SAT presents them.

The gap is one of depth and application. Students have surface-level awareness but cannot reliably apply rules independently. This module is designed to close that gap.

Research Questions This Study Examines

This module is part of a research study. Two questions guide the design:

  • To what extent do 11th grade students at Best High School demonstrate improved ability to independently identify grammar errors in passage-based contexts after completing a self-paced learning module?
  • How do 11th grade students describe their experience with immediate corrective feedback while completing SAT grammar practice activities within the module?

What the Research Says About Feedback

Studies consistently show that immediate corrective feedback — given right at the point of practice — supports skill building more than delayed feedback (Hattie & Timperley, 2007; Shute, 2008). Feedback that explains why an answer is correct or incorrect produces stronger learning gains than simple right/wrong feedback (Wisniewski et al., 2020).

Every question in this module gives you feedback right away, with an explanation of how the word functions in context — not just a checkmark.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Identify all eight parts of speech within sentences
  • Explain the function of a word in context, not just label it
  • Apply parts of speech knowledge to identify and correct SAT-style grammar errors
  • Demonstrate mastery on a summative assessment with items that mirror the SAT format

What Is a Noun?

A noun names a person, place, thing, or idea. Nouns can be concrete (things you can touch) or abstract (ideas you can't).

The committee reviewed the evidence with care.

Committee = group of people · evidence = thing · care = abstract idea

SAT Watch: The SAT sometimes tests whether you're using a noun when you need a verb or adverb. Ask: is this word naming something, or is it doing/describing something?

What Is a Pronoun?

A pronoun replaces a noun so we don't repeat it. The noun it replaces is called the antecedent. Pronouns must match their antecedent in number and case.

Subject Pronouns
Used as the subject
I, he, she, we, they, who
Object Pronouns
After verbs or prepositions
me, him, her, us, them, whom
Possessive
Show ownership
my, his, her, our, their, its
Indefinite
Non-specific people/things
everyone, each, neither, one
SAT Watch (connects to Q1–5): "Each of the researchers _____ required" — the subject is each, which is always singular. Use is, not are. Strip away the prepositional phrase to find the real subject.

Verb Types

Action Verb
Physical or mental action
run, think, conducted, studied
Linking Verb
Connects subject to description
is, are, seems, felt, went, became
Helping Verb
Assists main verb, shows tense
have, has, had, will, should, can

Verb Tense — The SAT Tests These Specifically

Four tenses come up in your pre/post assessments. Know the difference:

Simple past: They studied the effects last year. (one completed action)
Present perfect: Since 2015, researchers have studied the effects. (ongoing from past to now — use with "since")
Past perfect: By the time we arrived, they had left already. (action completed before another past action — use with "by the time")
Irregular past participle: The team has begun research. (not "began" — "has" requires the past participle)
Signal words matter: "Since" → present perfect (have/has + verb). "By the time" → past perfect (had + verb). "Has/Have" → needs past participle (begun, not began).

Subject-Verb Agreement

The verb must agree with its subject in number — not with the nearest noun.

❌ The collection of artifacts are on display. → ✓ The collection is on display.
❌ Each of the researchers are required... → ✓ Each of the researchers is required...
Strategy: Cross out the prepositional phrase. "The collection [of artifacts from various civilizations]" → subject = collection → singular → is.

Adjectives vs. Adverbs — Most-Tested Modifier Error

Adjective: describes a noun or pronoun. Adverb: describes a verb, adjective, or another adverb.

The scientists conducted experiments carefully. → "carefully" modifies the verb "conducted" → adverb ✓
After reviewing applications, the committee felt confident. → "felt" is a linking verb → use adjective ✓
The presentation went well. → "went" is a linking verb here → "well" = adjective describing result ✓
She felt bad about missing the meeting. → "felt" = linking verb → adjective "bad," not adverb "badly" ✓
Linking verb rule: After linking verbs (feel, seem, look, smell, taste, go, become), use an adjective, not an adverb. "The soup smells good" ✓ (not "goodly").

Misplaced Modifiers

An opening descriptive phrase must refer to the subject directly after the comma. If it doesn't, it's a dangling modifier.

❌ Having completed research, the paper was submitted. (The paper didn't complete the research.)
✓ Having completed research, the researchers submitted the paper. ✓
SAT strategy: When a sentence opens with a verb-phrase (Having _____, Running _____, After ______), the subject right after the comma must be the person/thing doing that action.

Prepositions

A preposition shows the relationship between a noun/pronoun and another word — often location, time, or direction. It always leads a prepositional phrase.

The collection of artifacts from various civilizations is on display.

"Of artifacts from various civilizations" is a prepositional phrase — strip it away to find the true subject of the sentence.

Common prepositions: of, in, on, by, for, with, from, about, between, through, during, despite, among, since, until.

Conjunctions — SAT Tests These Constantly

Coordinating (FANBOYS)
Join equal parts
for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so
Subordinating
Join dependent to main clause
because, although, since, when, if
Conjunctive Adverbs
Need semicolon before them
however, therefore, moreover
The study was expensive, but it provided valuable data. → "but" shows contrast ✓
The data was incomplete, but scientists continued collecting. → shows contrast despite obstacle ✓
Although the sample was small, it was statistically significant. → concession ✓
The experiment failed; however, researchers learned valuable lessons. → semicolon before "however" ✓
Punctuation rule: "The study succeeded, and it was published." ✓ — comma before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses. No comma needed after "and."
